Sheet 1 — the problem

A dynamo cannot make the flight longer

The brief asked for a wind-driven generator to charge the aircraft and extend its endurance. That is the one thing it cannot do.

A turbine takes its power out of the airstream, and that extraction appears as drag. Making 19.3 W electrically costs 29.8 W of shaft power — at 20 m/s, about 1.49 N of extra drag, roughly a tenth of the aircraft's weight. Sink rate goes from 0.52 m/s to 5.6. You always put in more than you get back.

AirspeedPower in the discShaft Electrical outExtraction drag Rotor

So the turbine here is a regenerative recovery device with a hard control law, not a charger.

It runs only when the energy is genuinely surplus. Three honest jobs, and the third is the one that earns its 126 grams.

Mode A — surplus lift

4 – 19 W

At the altitude ceiling with lift still coming up. Needs a 1.3–5.6 m/s thermal to break even, so it is opportunistic, not a power plan.

Mode B — regenerative descent

0.48 Wh / 500 m

Any commanded descent. Modest, but it is energy you were going to throw away as drag — and the turbine replaces the spoilers the wing would otherwise need.

Mode C — emergency power

8.1 W at 15 m/s

Against a 7.9 W hotel load. With a dead pack and a dead array it keeps the autopilot, GNSS and telemetry alive the whole way down. This is why airliners carry one.

Sheet 2 — the deployment mast

VDM-1: straight down, on one micro actuator

A pivoting arm swings the turbine's mass aft as it deploys and moves the centre of gravity 13.0 mm — 7.2 % of mean chord, most of the static margin. A mast that only goes down moves it nothing.

The stroke falls straight out of the rotor and is not negotiable: 75 mm of rotor radius, plus 30 mm of clearance to keep the disc out of the fuselage's flow field, plus 23 mm for the stowed package — 128 mm, whatever else changes.

That is the whole problem. The fuselage is 73 mm deep at this station, so a single sliding mast would stand 125 mm proud of the deck when retracted. Telescoping is not a refinement here, it is the only way the travel fits. Three stages nest to 70.7 mm and the reduction chain means the actuator only has to move 42.7 mm.

Choosing the actuator

The design load is 10.7 N — joint friction from the air load reacted as bushing couples, plus the moving mass under a 3 g retract, times 1.5 for stiction. Through the 1:3 chain that is 41 N at the actuator.

Two things that will bite The rotor's once-per-revolution imbalance runs to 133 Hz at the top of the operating band. Round tubes put the mast's first bending mode right on it; rectangular sections deep in the fore-aft direction — where the air load actually is — move it to 496 Hz, a factor of 3.7 clear. Second: the actuator's lead screw is the only thing holding the mast in flight, so it must be a self-locking ratio, verified with power off and the full air load applied.

Sheet 3 — where the hours come from

Solar, plus knowing how to find a thermal

A 39.8 W array of 11.33 cell equivalents covers the wing's upper surface. Cells are cut into thirds so the 125 mm dimension runs spanwise — the only curvature a strip has to follow is the 41.7 mm chordwise one, which is why the aerofoil's upper surface is held to a 400 mm minimum radius through the array band.

Powered level flight costs 15.1 W. The avionics and payload cost 7.9 W whether the motor runs or not — which means on this aircraft a watt saved in the electronics is worth more than a drag count.

Autonomous thermal soaring is the margin that absorbs a hazy day. It is not exotic: ArduPlane's variometer-based soaring controller does it in tree, and it is the difference between meeting the requirement and meeting it comfortably.

Sheet 5 — what it sees

Sub-pixel fire detection

At 400 m the thermal core resolves 2.71 m per pixel — far coarser than any fire worth catching early. That does not matter, because radiant exitance goes as T⁴.

A flame filling one percent of a pixel still drags that pixel's apparent temperature up by tens of kelvin. The figures below come from integrating Planck's law over the 8–14 µm band, which captures only 17 % of an 800 K flame's emission against 38 % of the background's — LWIR is the unfavourable band for small hot fires, and this already accounts for it.

The sensor is not the limit — clutter is Sun-baked rock, dark soil and asphalt reach 55–70 °C on a summer afternoon: +28 to +43 K. Anything below that is indistinguishable from hot ground in a single frame, which puts the real detection floor at roughly 0.5–1 m² of active flame, not the 0.05 m² the raw sensor noise implies. Getting under it needs contextual thresholding against each pixel's own neighbourhood, persistence across frames, and a visible-camera plume cross-check.

Sheet 6 — what the mechanism costs

Retraction is right, but it is not transformative

Folding the pods away is worth less than intuition suggests. Flying with both hanging out costs only 6.7 % in sink rate, because at soaring lift coefficients induced drag dominates and a parasite-drag increment gets diluted.

The turbine has to retract regardless — it hangs 222 mm below the keel and there is no landing gear. The motor is a genuine choice, and the honest answer is that a plain nose-mounted folding prop wins on the numbers:

The nose variant is 71 g lighter with 4.6 % less sink and nothing to jam, and the sensor turret is slung under the nose rather than on it, so the nose is free. The dorsal pylon buys three things back: prop clearance on a belly landing, a nose kept free for a forward-looking sensor later, and a thrust line above the drag line so power changes pitch the aircraft less. The baseline keeps the pylon because that is what was asked for and the penalty is small and real. If endurance is the only thing that matters, build the nose variant.
