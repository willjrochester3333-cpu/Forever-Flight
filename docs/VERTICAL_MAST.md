# VDM-1 — vertical deployment mast

> Generated by `tools/mast.py`. Re-run after any change to `tools/config.py`.

A telescoping vertical mast that lowers the energy-recovery turbine **128 mm** straight down, driven by one micro linear actuator moving **42.7 mm**. It replaces the baseline swing arm (see [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md) §6).


## 1. Why bother — the swing arm's real problem

A pivoting arm carries the turbine's mass through an arc. On the FF-1 the pod travels 157 mm aft between stowed and deployed, and that moves the centre of gravity **+12.9 mm — +7.1 % of mean chord**. Static margin falls from 20 % to 13 % the moment the turbine deploys, which is at the aft CG limit.


A mast that only moves vertically shifts the CG by **nothing**. Two other things come free with it:


- **The rotor axis stays aligned with the flow through the whole travel.** A swing arm at mid-stroke has the rotor at 45° to the airstream, which is a large asymmetric blade load exactly when the mechanism is least supported. That is what limits the speed at which you can move a swing arm.
- **Deployment depth becomes a continuous variable.** The mast can be part-extended, so the turbine's exposure — and therefore the drag it costs — can be modulated instead of being on or off.


What it costs is mass and parts: **+90 g**, taking sink rate from 0.524 to 0.542 m/s (+3.4 %). Endurance still closes above eight hours.


## 2. The stroke is set by the rotor, and nothing else

|  |  |
|---|---|
| Rotor radius | 75 mm |
| Disc clearance from the body | 30 mm (0.20 × rotor Ø) |
| Stowed package radius | 23 mm (nacelle + blades folded aft over it) |
| **Required stroke** | **128 mm** |


That sum is the whole sizing problem, and it does not care about stage count, station or fuselage depth — move any of those and the stroke stays at 128 mm. The only lever is the rotor, and shrinking it is not available: turbine power goes as R², so dropping to a 110 mm rotor costs 46 % of the output and takes Mode C (emergency power) below the hotel load, which is the mode that justifies carrying the turbine at all.


Clearance is the one soft number. 0.20 × diameter keeps the disc out of the fuselage boundary layer (about 12 mm at this station) and out of the worst of the flow curvature under the belly. Tightening it to 0.12 would save 12 mm of stroke at the cost of a non-uniform inflow across the disc — 1-per-rev blade loading, lost C_p, and noise.


## 3. Why it has to telescope

The fuselage is **73 mm deep** at station x = 425 mm (deck 38.5, keel -34.5). A single sliding mast needs its own length plus the stroke: retracted, its top ends up about 128 mm above where its deployed top was, so it would stand roughly 125 mm proud of the deck. There is nowhere to put that.


**Telescoping is not a refinement here — it is the only way 128 mm of travel fits inside 73 mm of airframe.** Stage count then trades three ways: more stages nest shorter and need less actuator stroke, but multiply the input force and add joint lash.


| Stages | Travel per stage (mm) | Nested height (mm) | Ventral fairing (mm) | vs 73 mm available |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 128.0 | 204.0 | 169.0 | **131 mm too tall** |
| 2 | 64.0 | 104.0 | 69.0 | **31 mm too tall** |
| 3 | 42.7 | 70.7 | 35.7 | fits |
| 4 | 32.0 | 54.0 | 19.0 | fits |
| 5 | 25.6 | 44.0 | 9.0 | fits |


**Three stages.** Two will not nest; four fits but drives the actuator force past what a micro actuator delivers.


## 4. Mast

|  |  |
|---|---|
| Station | x = 425 mm, on the wing trailing-edge bulkhead |
| Sections (fore-aft × lateral) | 34.0×22.0 → 30.4×18.4 → 26.8×14.8 → 23.2×11.2 mm |
| Wall | 0.8 mm carbon |
| Stage length | 66.7 mm |
| Retained overlap | 24.0 mm at full extension |
| Nested height | 70.7 mm (fits under the deck) |
| Nacelle travel | z -47.2 → -175.2 mm |
| Deployed rotor disc | z -100 to -250 mm (216 mm below the keel) |
| Ventral fairing | 35.7 mm deep |


**The sections are rectangular, deep fore-aft.** Three reasons, and the first is the one that decides it:

1. The air load is fore-aft, so that is the axis that needs the second moment. Round tubes of the same mass put the mast's first bending mode on top of the rotor's 1-per-rev — see §6.
2. A non-circular section reacts the generator's torque without a keyway or a splined joint.
3. The flat faces are where the racks go.


## 5. Drive

```
micro linear actuator  (horizontal, along the fuselage)
        |
        |  1:1 bellcrank, 90 deg offset, equal arms
        v
stage 1 head  (Scotch-yoke slot -> pure vertical translation)
        |
        |  rack - pinion - rack doubler
        v
stage 2   (2 x stage 1)
        |
        |  rack - pinion - rack doubler
        v
stage 3   (3 x stage 1)  ->  nacelle, generator, rotor
```


A pinion carried on stage 1 meshes with a rack fixed to the trunk and a rack on stage 2, so stage 2 moves twice stage 1. A second pinion on stage 2 meshes with racks on stages 1 and 3, giving 3×. Module 0.4, Ø8 mm pinions.


Rack and pinion rather than cable reeving on purpose: it drives **positively in both directions** with one element per joint, there is nothing to tension or re-tension, and there are no dead centres. A reeved telescope needs a separate extend and retract cable per stage — four cables and four sheaves here — and every one of them is a creep-and-stretch item.


An equal-armed bellcrank with its arms 90° apart gives exactly 1:1 between the actuator's horizontal travel and the yoke's vertical travel, for any sweep angle. Arms 30 mm, pivot at x = 447 mm.

### 5.2 Actuator selection

Class figures for common micro linear actuators. **Verify against the current datasheet before ordering** — these are the numbers to size against, not a price list.


| Actuator class | Stroke (mm) | Force (N) | Mass (g) | Body (mm) | Deploy (s) | Margin |  |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PQ12-P 100:1 | 20 | 50 | 15 | 44 | 7.1 | 1.2× | stroke short |
| L12-30 100:1 | 30 | 45 | 34 | 81 | 3.3 | 1.1× | stroke short |
| L12-30 210:1 | 30 | 80 | 34 | 81 | 7.1 | 1.9× | stroke short |
| L12-50 100:1 | 50 | 45 | 40 | 101 | 3.3 | 1.1× | thin margin |
| L12-50 210:1 | 50 | 80 | 40 | 101 | 7.1 | 1.9× | thin margin |
| L12-100 210:1 | 100 | 80 | 56 | 151 | 7.1 | 1.9× | thin margin |
| L16-50 63:1 | 50 | 150 | 84 | 128 | 3.3 | 3.7× | too heavy |


**L12-50 at 210:1.** 41 N needed against 80 N available — and the design load already carries a 1.5× stiction factor, so the margin on the physical load is nearer 3×. Deployment takes about 7 s, which is fine: nothing about deploying this turbine is time-critical, including Mode C.


If the build comes in heavy or the bushings measure worse than μ = 0.12, the fix is a **1.4:1 bellcrank with an L12-100**: the actuator then moves 60 mm for the same 42.7 mm at the yoke and the force requirement drops to about 29 N — 2.7× margin, for 16 g.


> **The actuator's lead screw is the only thing holding the mast in flight.** It must be a self-locking ratio (100:1 or 210:1, not 50:1), and that has to be verified on the bench with power removed and the full air load applied to the extended mast — not taken from the datasheet.


## 6. Structure and resonance

|  |  |
|---|---|
| First bending mode | 496 Hz |
| Rotor 1-per-rev at the top of the band | 133 Hz (7958 rpm at 25 m/s) |
| **Frequency ratio** | **3.7×** (want > 1.5) |
| Tip deflection, elastic | 0.00 mm under the air load |
| Tip deflection, joint lash | 0.94 mm at 0.04 mm per side |


This is the trap in the whole design. An unbalanced rotor is a rotating force at 1P, and a cantilevered mast carrying it is a tuning fork. Round tubes of comparable mass put the first mode within a few hertz of 1P — dead on resonance across the normal operating band. The rectangular sections move it clear by a factor of 3.7.


Back it up in the controller anyway. The generator's field-oriented controller sets the electrical load and therefore the rotor speed, so an **RPM avoid-band costs nothing** — exclude ±8 % around any measured resonance and let the controller step over it. Measure the real frequency with a ping test on the assembled, extended mast before the first spin-up; do not trust the calculation.


## 7. Control, interlocks and failure

The turbine control law in [DESIGN.md](DESIGN.md) §6.1 is unchanged. The mast adds these:


| Rule | Why |
|---|---|
| Do not move the mast above 20 m/s | Air load and bushing friction both scale with V²; above this the actuator margin goes |
| Brake the rotor electrically before retracting | Short the generator phases through the controller. A spinning rotor entering the bay is a blade strike, and it is gyroscopic |
| Full retract below 25 m AGL, as a hardware failsafe | The rotor hangs 216 mm below the keel and there is no landing gear. Wire it independent of the scripting engine |
| Two end sensors, not one | The actuator's own potentiometer plus a Hall sensor at each end of the mast travel. An interlock on a single sensor is not an interlock |
| Magnetic detent at stowed | 2 x 4 N magnetic, stowed — stops the stack rattling and creeping without adding a breakout mechanism |


**If the actuator fails with the mast extended, the aircraft still has to land.** Design the trunk-to-bulkhead joint as the weak link: a defined shear load that lets the mast break away cleanly on a belly landing, with the mast on a lanyard so it does not become debris. That confines the damage to two cheap parts. A spring-loaded fail-safe retract was considered and rejected — a spring strong enough to retract against friction adds its full force to every deployment, and the actuator margin will not carry it.


## 8. Build and rigging notes

1. Bond the trunk between two bulkheads, not into the shell. You are cutting a 34 × 22 mm hole through the keel at the station carrying the wing's rear load path — it needs a frame, and a carbon rim around the opening.
2. Assemble and cycle the mast **dry, out of the aircraft, 200 times** before it goes in. Anything that will bind, binds early.
3. Set the rack mesh with the stages fully extended, then check backlash retracted. It is the extended end that carries load.
4. Rig the bellcrank so the yoke bottoms on its hard stop 0.5 mm before the actuator reaches its own end of travel — the stop takes the load, not the screw.
5. Fit the wiper seal at the trunk mouth last, and check it does not add measurable breakout force. An open trunk is a ram-air path straight into the fuselage.
6. Ping-test the extended mast and record the frequency. Compare with the predicted 496 Hz before the first spin-up.
